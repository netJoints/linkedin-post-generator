Yes. Build the swipeable cards first.

A picture can help a LinkedIn post. A generated photograph is the weak version of that idea. The posts people actually swipe are designed cards: a hook, one idea per card, a checklist, and a close. LinkedIn shows them as a document you click through, or as a multi-image post. Both are made on this laptop from the draft you already write, with no image API and no LinkedIn login.

What you have been seeing in the feed

Those postcard slides with a next arrow and a row of dots are a native document post. You upload one PDF, PPTX, or DOCX. LinkedIn paginates it. Their help page caps the file at 100 MB and 300 pages, and it says video and animation inside the document are dropped and shown as still pages. A short video is a second file, made from the same cards, and uploaded as a video post.

Company pages: documents and multi-image still lead

Across 1.3 million LinkedIn business posts, native documents were the top format for 2025 at 7.00% average engagement, up from 6.10% in 2024. By Q3 2026, documents and multi-image posts were tied at 6.40%, and both still sat well above a single image, text, and links. Video fell harder than the swipe formats.

Personal profiles: the carousel wins reach, a single image wins rate

This app writes in a practitioner’s voice, so the personal-profile study is the closer match. On that set, a document is the only written format that beats a typical post on both reach and engagement count. A single image has the highest engagement rate. Video’s median reach fell from 1,157 impressions to 740 over the year. The long article is the weakest feed format in the set.

A third study agrees on the ranking, not on the rate

Buffer’s 2026 format study, covering tens of millions of posts, puts LinkedIn carousels at a 21.77% median engagement rate, then video at 7.35%, images at 6.52%, and text at 3.18%. That rate uses Buffer’s sample and formula, so it is not drawn on the chart above. LinkedIn’s own creator guidance also pushes video. The measured engagement still favors the swipeable document, which is why the video is a free second file rather than the thing this app builds first.

The card sequence that gets swiped to the end

Guides that track this format in 2026 converge on the same shape. Six to ten pages. Portrait, because a phone is where most of the feed is read. One idea per page, under about 40 words. Body type large enough to read at a third of the design size. The first card earns the swipe. The second-to-last card is the one people save. The last card asks for one action.

Click through the frame. It is a template, not a generated post. The words on each card come from the article and the short post this app writes, and only from claims those drafts were allowed to make.

Other products already do the pretty version

The market is crowded with cloud carousel makers. They take a topic or a URL and return a designed PDF, sometimes an MP4. Prices for the AI tier cluster around $15 to $69 a month, and the free tiers watermark the export. None of them is this product: a local model, sources you supplied, and a refusal to invent a number.

What gets built, in order

The deck on the project home page is the motion reference: next, previous, a page count, and a progress mark, on a 1600×900 stage. That stage is 16:9, which is the wrong shape for a phone feed. The cards keep the interaction and use a 1080×1350 canvas. The words are HTML, not an image model. Diffusion models still misspell, and Ollama’s experimental image generation was removed on 4 Aug 2026 and had not returned as of the 12 Oct 2026 release notes.

Document post · upload this

Flattened PDF, every page 1080×1350, RGB, ideally under 10 MB. LinkedIn accepts PDF, PPT, PPTX, DOC, and DOCX up to 100 MB and 300 pages. PDF is the format that renders consistently. Give the document a title. You upload it with the short post as the caption.

Safe type: hook around 80–110 px, body at least 36 px, nothing under 28 px, margins of about 80 px. Zoom the preview to a third and read it there.

Video post · second file

LinkedIn’s organic video rules: MP4, from 3 seconds on desktop to 15 minutes, 75 KB to 5 GB, aspect ratio between 1:2.4 and 2.4:1. A 4:5 frame at 1080×1350 and 30 fps sits inside that and fills a phone. Keep words off the edges, where the player chrome sits.

Do not expect the PDF itself to animate. LinkedIn’s document help says animations are shown as stills. The motion lives only in the MP4.

What would not earn the likes

A generated scene with a caption baked into the pixels. A 16:9 export of the existing product deck. A link post, which is the lowest format on the company-page chart, at 2.50% in Q3 2026. A card that states a percentage the sources never gave you. Any of those can be built. They are not the posts that get saved.
